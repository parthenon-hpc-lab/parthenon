\title{\bf The Parthenon Loop Abstraction:\\
A Portable, Compile-Time-Selectable Loop Structure\\
for Block-Structured Kernels}
\author{Parthenon developers}
\date{Draft --- \today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Block-structured finite-volume kernels have a portability problem that is not
solved by a portable parallel-for alone: the \emph{loop structure} that performs
well on a CPU is genuinely different from the one that performs well on a GPU,
even when the arithmetic is identical. The Parthenon loop abstraction
(\code{src/loop_abstraction}) lets a kernel be written once against a logical
$(\text{block}, k, j, i)$ iteration space and then be compiled into one of
several concrete loop structures, selected by compile-time tags. This document
motivates the abstraction with a concrete reconstruct-then-Riemann flux kernel,
shows how a performance engineer would hand-write that kernel differently for CPU
and GPU, and then develops the abstraction's vocabulary --- index spaces, loop
tags, inner tags, backends, scratch, halos, and pack views --- as the machinery
that expresses both hand-written forms from a single source.
\end{abstract}

% =====================================================================
\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

Parthenon kernels iterate over a logical index space of cells indexed by
$(\text{block}, k, j, i)$. The existing \code{par_for} / \code{par_for_outer}
wrappers make the \emph{dispatch} portable: the same call compiles to a flat
Kokkos range on a GPU and to a nested loop on a CPU. What they do not abstract is
the \emph{shape} of the computation --- how work is chunked, where intermediate
results are stored versus recomputed, and where the boundaries between levels of
parallelism fall. For simple pointwise kernels this does not matter. For the
reconstruction/flux kernels at the heart of a Godunov hydro scheme it matters a
great deal, and the best shape is not the same on both architectures.

The loop abstraction exists to close that gap. A kernel declares \emph{what}
index space it covers and \emph{how} its variables are laid out; a small set of
compile-time tags then selects the concrete loop structure. The intent is that a
single kernel body can be compiled into either a CPU-friendly hierarchical form
that computes intermediate results once and stores them, or a GPU-friendly flat
form that recomputes them on demand, with no change to the physics code.

This document is organized around that claim. Section~\ref{sec:motivation}
develops a single motivating kernel and writes it out by hand twice, once for each
architecture, to make the differences concrete. Section~\ref{sec:onekernel} shows
the same kernel written once in the abstraction and recovers both hand-written
forms by changing a tag. The remaining sections
(\ref{sec:indexspace}--\ref{sec:views}) develop each piece of the vocabulary in
turn, and Section~\ref{sec:halos} treats halos --- the mechanism for
producer/consumer reuse --- in detail, including their behavior in reduced
dimensionality.

\paragraph{Why this is not a reinvention of Kokkos.} Kokkos is a general
performance-portability layer: it can express essentially any parallel pattern,
and it makes very few assumptions about what a kernel does. That generality is a
strength, but it also means the programmer is responsible for choosing and hand-
coding the loop structure, and a generic pattern gives the compiler and library
little to exploit. The loop abstraction takes the opposite stance. It is
deliberately \emph{narrower} --- it targets block-structured
$(\text{block}, k, j, i)$ finite-volume kernels and a handful of loop shapes that
are known to perform well for them --- and it is built \emph{on top of} Kokkos,
not in place of it. The abstraction still dispatches through Kokkos (or a raw host
nest) underneath; what it adds is a vocabulary specialized enough that the library
can make the structural decisions a programmer would otherwise make by hand:
where to chunk, what to store versus recompute, which index is contiguous, how
scratch is sized. The trade-off is explicit: because the abstraction only knows
how to express a constrained family of kernels, it cannot describe an arbitrary
computation the way raw Kokkos can. In exchange, within that family it is easier
to write a kernel that is already close to optimal on both architectures, and to
switch between structures without rewriting the kernel. It is a domain-specific
convenience layer over Kokkos, not a competitor to it.

\section{A motivating example: a finite-volume flux kernel}
\label{sec:motivation}
% =====================================================================

Consider the flux computation in a directionally-split Godunov scheme, for the
$x_1$ sweep. For each cell we need the flux $F_{i-1/2}$ on its lower $x_1$ face.
The standard recipe is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Reconstruct}, for each cell $i$, the two one-sided interface states on
  its faces. A reconstruction reads a stencil of cell-centered values around cell
  $i$ --- e.g.\ $q_{i-1}, q_i, q_{i+1}$ for a three-point scheme --- forms a single
  slope, and applies it toward each face, giving the state $q^{+}_i$ extrapolated to
  the cell's upper face ($i+1/2$) and $q^{-}_i$ to its lower face ($i-1/2$). Both
  states come from the \emph{same} stencil read and slope, so a cell naturally
  produces them together.
\item \textbf{Solve a Riemann problem} at each face from the two states that meet
  there --- the upper-face state of the cell below and the lower-face state of the
  cell above: $F_{i-1/2} = \mathcal{R}\!\left(q^{+}_{i-1}, q^{-}_i\right)$.
\end{enumerate}

The key structural observation is that \emph{a cell's two states are computed from
one shared slope}, and that slope is then needed at \emph{both} of the cell's faces:
the Riemann solve above cell $i$ reads its $q^{+}_i$, and the one below reads its
$q^{-}_i$. Whether it is worth reconstructing each cell once and \emph{storing} both
states so the two flanking Riemann solves can read them, or simply
\emph{recomputing} a cell's slope separately at each face that needs it, is precisely
the decision that differs between CPU and GPU. The next two subsections write the
kernel out both ways.

Throughout, $q$ is a cell-centered field, and we compute the $x_1$ flux
$F$ on $x_1$-faces.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{How you would write it for a CPU}
\label{sec:cpu}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

On a CPU the dominant concerns are (i) keeping the innermost loop long,
unit-stride, and vectorizable, and (ii) not repeating expensive arithmetic. Both
push toward a \emph{hierarchical} structure: an outer loop that walks planes or
pencils of the block, and an inner loop that sweeps contiguously along $x_1$.
Reconstruction is done \emph{once per cell} and both of that cell's states are
stored in scratch; the flux loop then reads the two states that meet at each face.

\begin{lstlisting}
// CPU-style: reconstruct each cell once into scratch, then solve the fluxes.
for (int k = kb.s; k <= kb.e; ++k) {
  for (int j = jb.s; j <= jb.e; ++j) {

    // scratch spans [is-1, ie]: one extra cell on the low side, because the
    // flux at face i reads the upper-face state q^+ of the cell at i-1.
    Real *qp = scratch_plus_row(k, j);   // q^+ (upper-face state) of each cell
    Real *qm = scratch_minus_row(k, j);  // q^- (lower-face state) of each cell

    // Producer: reconstruct every cell in the (extended) row exactly once. One
    // call forms the cell's slope and writes both one-sided states through the
    // trailing out-pointers.
    const Real *q_row = &q(k, j, 0);
    #pragma omp simd
    for (int i = is - 1; i <= ie; ++i) {
      reconstruct(q_row[i-1], q_row[i], q_row[i+1], &qp[i], &qm[i]);
    }

    // Consumer: one Riemann solve per face, from the two cells' states that meet
    // there -- the upper-face state of cell i-1 and the lower-face state of cell i.
    Real *F_row = &F(k, j, 0);
    #pragma omp simd
    for (int i = is; i <= ie; ++i) {
      F_row[i] = riemann(qp[i-1], qm[i]);
    }
  }
}
\end{lstlisting}

Three features matter and will reappear as abstraction concepts:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Two loops over nearly the same range.} The producer runs over
  $[i_s-1, i_e]$ and the consumer over $[i_s, i_e]$. The producer's range is the
  consumer's range \emph{plus one extra cell on the low side}, because
  \code{riemann(qp[i-1], qm[i])} reads a produced value at $i-1$. That ``one
  extra cell'' is exactly what a \emph{halo} will name (Section~\ref{sec:halos}).
\item \textbf{Compute-once, read-twice \emph{work}.} Each cell's reconstruction is
  done once and stored, but its \emph{slope} feeds the fluxes on both of the cell's
  faces (the flux above reads $q^+$, the flux below reads $q^-$). Storing spends the
  stencil read and slope once per cell; the flat form below repeats them at each
  face. Each stored state itself is read exactly once --- it is the per-cell
  \emph{work}, not any one value, that is shared. The scratch buffer is sized to the
  extended producer range.
\item \textbf{Contiguous inner loops.} Both inner loops are unit-stride in $i$
  and marked for SIMD; \code{q_row}, \code{qp}, \code{qm} are raw pointers so the
  compiler sees \code{a[i]}-style access. The row shown here is one $i$-pencil, but
  nothing forbids fusing several adjacent $j$- (or $k$-) rows into one longer
  contiguous run to give the vectorizer more to chew on. Because the allocated
  block is contiguous in memory, such a fused run can be walked as a single span;
  the cost is a little extra ghost work where the run crosses row boundaries. The
  abstraction exposes this as a tunable chunk size (Section~\ref{sec:looptags});
  the point to plant now is that ``how much to fuse'' is a knob, not a fixed
  choice.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{How you would write it for a GPU}
\label{sec:gpu}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

On a GPU the concerns invert. Floating-point work is cheap relative to memory
traffic and synchronization, and a single flat grid of threads gives the
scheduler the most freedom to hide latency. Storing reconstructed states in
shared memory would force a team structure and a barrier between the producer and
consumer phases; it is usually cheaper to give each face its own thread and let it
\emph{recompute} whatever reconstructed states it needs, reading the input field
directly through the pack.

\begin{lstlisting}
// GPU-style: one thread per face, recompute reconstruction, no scratch.
par_for(is, ie, jb.s, jb.e, kb.s, kb.e,
  KOKKOS_LAMBDA(const int k, const int j, const int i) {
    // Face i needs cell i-1's upper-face state and cell i's lower-face state.
    // Each reconstruct forms one cell's slope and writes both its states; this
    // thread uses one of the two and discards the other (recomputed work).
    Real qp_im1, qm_im1;
    reconstruct(q(k,j,i-2), q(k,j,i-1), q(k,j,i), &qp_im1, &qm_im1);  // want qp_im1
    Real qp_i, qm_i;
    reconstruct(q(k,j,i-1), q(k,j,i), q(k,j,i+1), &qp_i, &qm_i);      // want qm_i
    F(k, j, i) = riemann(qp_im1, qm_i);
  });
\end{lstlisting}

Here cell $i-1$'s reconstruction is done twice: this thread runs it and keeps
$q^{+}_{i-1}$ (discarding $q^{-}_{i-1}$), and the thread owning face $i-1$ runs it
again and keeps $q^{-}_{i-1}$. Every cell is thus reconstructed by both of the
threads on its faces, roughly doubling the reconstruction arithmetic. That
redundancy is deliberate: it buys a flat, barrier-free, single-level parallel loop
with direct field access and no shared-memory bookkeeping.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What differs, and what does not}
\label{sec:differs}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Comparing the two, the \emph{physics} --- \code{reconstruct}, \code{riemann}, and
the fact that the flux at a face reads the upper-face state of the cell below and
the lower-face state of the cell above --- is byte-for-byte identical. What differs
is entirely structural:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{CPU form (\S\ref{sec:cpu})} & \textbf{GPU form (\S\ref{sec:gpu})} \\
 & \code{bvoi} / \code{bovi} & \code{boiv} \\
\midrule
Parallelism   & hierarchical (team over planes, & flat (one thread per face) \\
              & SIMD along $i$)                 &  \\
Intermediate  & reconstructed once per cell,    & recomputed at each face \\
              & stored in scratch               & (both faces redo the cell) \\
Access        & raw pointers, \code{a[i]}       & direct pack access \\
Barrier       & producer/consumer split         & none \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

An abstraction that lets us write the shared physics once and choose the column at
compile time is exactly what Section~\ref{sec:onekernel} delivers. At the level of
this section the choice is just the one made above: a \emph{hierarchical} form
that chunks the block and stores intermediates in scratch, versus a \emph{flat
pointwise} form that gives each cell its own thread and recomputes. Both are
selected by a single template argument on the \code{IndexSpace}, and the ``one
extra face'' the producer covers becomes a \code{minus_i} \emph{halo}. The named
tags that spell these choices, and the finer distinctions among the hierarchical
variants, are introduced in Section~\ref{sec:onekernel} alongside the code and
developed in Section~\ref{sec:looptags}.

\section{The same kernel, written once}
\label{sec:onekernel}
% =====================================================================

Here is the flux kernel expressed in the abstraction. It is written against an
\code{IndexSpace} whose tags are left as template parameters; instantiating those
tags selects the CPU or GPU structure from Section~\ref{sec:motivation} without
touching the body.

\begin{lstlisting}
namespace la = parthenon::loop_abstraction;

// recon_halo names the extra reconstructed cell the flux loop will read: the flux
// at face i reads the upper-face state q^+ of cell i-1, i.e. one cell in -i.
using recon_halo = la::halo::minus_i_t;

// F1-face index space over the interior, backend chosen by the tags. Iterating over
// F1 faces makes the index range include the topmost x1 face, so no flux is missed;
// each array still reads the index by its own centering (cell-centered for the
// reconstructed states, face for the flux).
using idx_space_t = la::IndexSpace<LOOP_TAG, INNER_TAG>;
idx_space_t idx_space(
    ninner, IndexDomain::interior, /*halo=*/0, pack.GetNBlocks(),
    md, TopologicalElement::F1);
idx_space.AddPerPointScratch<Real, recon_halo>(2);   // q^+ and q^- per cell

const auto dx1 = idx_space.GetDelta(X1DIR);           // logical +1 step in x1

la::outer(idx_space, KOKKOS_LAMBDA(const idx_space_t::idx_range_t &r, int b) {
  // Extend this slice by recon_halo: the producer below now covers the base
  // cells plus the one extra cell in -i.
  const auto rp = la::AddHalo<recon_halo>(r);
  auto qp = la::GetPerPointScratch<Real>(rp);
  auto qm = la::GetPerPointScratch<Real>(rp);
  auto pv = la::make_pack_view(rp, pack);

  // Producer: reconstruct each cell over the halo-extended range -- one call forms
  // the cell's slope and writes both one-sided states through the out-pointers.
  la::inner(rp, [&](auto f) {
    reconstruct(pv(q(), f - dx1), pv(q(), f), pv(q(), f + dx1), &qp(f), &qm(f));
  });
  r.TeamBarrier();  // producer must finish before the consumer reads (no-op where serial)

  // Consumer: one Riemann solve per face over the base range, from the two cells'
  // states that meet there -- cell i-1's upper-face q^+ and cell i's lower-face q^-.
  auto fv = la::make_flux_pack_view(r, pack, X1DIR);
  la::inner(r, [&](auto f) {
    fv(q(), f) = riemann(qp(f - dx1), qm(f));
  });
});
\end{lstlisting}

Now the instantiations:
\begin{itemize}
\item \code{IndexSpace<loop_tag::bovi, inner_tag::logical_flat>} (or
  \code{loop_tag::bvoi}) recovers the CPU form. \code{outer} walks chunks of the
  block; \code{inner} sweeps a contiguous span; \code{GetPerPointScratch} resolves
  to team scratch (Kokkos) or a host bump-arena buffer (raw), sized to the
  halo-extended producer range; the \code{TeamBarrier} separates producer and
  consumer. Each cell is reconstructed once into scratch and its two states are
  read by the flanking flux solves. Choosing \code{bvoi} instead keeps the whole
  block live per slice --- more scratch, often faster (Section~\ref{sec:looptags}).
\item \code{IndexSpace<loop_tag::boiv, ...>} recovers the GPU form. \code{outer}
  is a flat grid over all $(\text{block},k,j,i)$, scratch is a tiny per-thread
  stack buffer, and the \code{TeamBarrier} is a no-op. The producer and consumer
  run back-to-back on the same thread, so each cell's reconstruction is redone by
  both of the threads on its faces --- exactly Section~\ref{sec:gpu}. Note that here the word
  \code{inner} is something of a lie: for the base (non-halo) range the
  ``\code{inner} loop'' is a single logical cell --- a one-iteration loop, i.e.\
  not really a loop at all --- and even for the halo-extended range it runs only
  over the handful of halo offsets, not a sweep. Writing it as \code{inner}
  regardless is what lets the \emph{same} kernel text also compile to the
  hierarchical forms, where \code{inner} genuinely is a sweep; \code{boiv} is the
  degenerate limit in which that sweep collapses to one point.
\end{itemize}

One line in the body deserves emphasis, because it is where a large part of the
CPU/GPU performance difference is actually realized:
\code{make_pack_view(rp, pack)} adapts to the chosen structure. For the
hierarchical, flat-indexed paths (the CPU form) it pulls raw pointers out of the
\code{SparsePack} at construction, so \code{pv(q(), f)} compiles to an
\code{a[idx]}-style access the vectorizer can see through --- recovering the
raw-pointer access of Section~\ref{sec:cpu} without the kernel spelling any
pointer arithmetic. For the pointwise / coordinate paths
(\code{boiv}, \code{logical_coords}) it is instead a thin wrapper that forwards
each access straight to the sparse pack, which is what the GPU form wants. The
kernel text is identical either way; the view quietly does the right thing for the
tag. Section~\ref{sec:views} returns to this.

The flux kernel is the demanding case that motivated the design --- it exercises
scratch, halos, and a producer/consumer split all at once --- but it is not the
only intended use. The same \code{outer}/\code{inner} form is meant to be the way
ordinary block-structured kernels are written too, including ones that touch no
scratch and involve no stencil at all: a pointwise equation-of-state call, a
per-cell source term, a copy or a rescale. Such a kernel is simply the flux
example with the producer loop and halo removed --- one \code{inner} over the base
range --- and it still benefits from compile-time structure selection and the
pack-view access described above. The goal is a single idiom for block kernels,
with the reconstruction/flux machinery as the elaborate end of a spectrum whose
simple end is an unadorned loop.

That full kernel used several pieces at once --- tags, a halo, scratch, offsets,
pack views --- without defining any of them. The next section strips the example
back to its bare skeleton and names the components; the subsections after it then
develop each component in turn, adding the omitted pieces (offsets, scratch, halos)
back onto the running example as they are introduced.

% =====================================================================
\section{Anatomy of a loop-abstraction kernel}
\label{sec:anatomy}
% =====================================================================

Stripped of scratch, halos, and neighbor access, every loop-abstraction kernel has
the same three-part skeleton: declare an \code{IndexSpace}, launch \code{outer}
over it, and run \code{inner} inside. Here is the smallest complete example --- a
pointwise kernel that writes one value per cell, with no scratch, no halo, and no
stencil:
\begin{lstlisting}
namespace la = parthenon::loop_abstraction;

// (1) The index space: WHAT to iterate (interior cells of nblocks blocks, laid
// out per md) and HOW to compile it (the two tags). CC = cell-centered.
using idx_space_t = la::IndexSpace<la::loop_tag::bovi, la::inner_tag::logical_flat>;
idx_space_t idx_space(
    ninner, IndexDomain::interior, /*halo=*/0, nblocks, md, TopologicalElement::CC);

// (2) outer() launches the outer loop. Its body is called with an
// InnerIndexRange -- one slice of the index space -- and the block index b.
// idx_space_t::idx_range_t names that slice type (no decltype needed).
la::outer(idx_space, KOKKOS_LAMBDA(const idx_space_t::idx_range_t &r, int b) {
  // (3) inner() runs the inner loop over that slice, calling the body once per
  // logical cell. The body may take a single opaque index...
  la::inner(r, [&](auto idx) {
    out(idx) = source(idx);
  });
});
\end{lstlisting}

The named pieces, each the subject of a subsection below, are:
\begin{description}
\item[\codelbl{IndexSpace<loop\_tag, inner\_tag, backend, reduction>}] The object
  that fixes the iteration space, the memory layout, and --- through its compile-time
  tags --- the loop structure that will be generated. It is what \code{outer} is
  called on. Its index information and coordinate conventions are covered in
  Section~\ref{sec:indexspace}; the first three tags in Sections~\ref{sec:looptags}
  (\code{loop_tag}), \ref{sec:innertags} (\code{inner_tag}), and
  \ref{sec:backends} (\code{backend}). The trailing \code{reduction} parameter
  defaults to a non-reducing space and is left off in the ordinary
  \mbox{three-parameter} form; it is only supplied for the reduction paths
  (Section~\ref{sec:reductions}).
\item[\codelbl{outer(idx\_space, body)}] Launches the outermost loop. The body is
  a \code{KOKKOS_LAMBDA} (it is stored and invoked on the device) taking an
  \code{InnerIndexRange} and the block index.
\item[\codelbl{InnerIndexRange} (the ``inner range'', \codelbl{r})] One slice of
  the index space handed to the outer body --- what a ``slice'' is depends on the
  loop tag (a whole block, one chunk, or a single cell; see
  Section~\ref{sec:innerrange}). It is the object \code{inner} runs over, and it
  knows how to translate between the flat, memory, and $(k,j,i)$ index forms
  (\code{r.GetKJI(idx)}).
\item[\codelbl{inner(r, body)}] Runs the inner loop over one slice, calling the
  body once per logical cell.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Body signatures.} The inner body may be written in two forms. The
opaque form \code{[\&](auto idx)} receives whatever index the inner tag produces
(a flat integer, an \code{Index3}, or a memory offset); it is the form intended for
normal use, and the one that composes with the offset arithmetic and pack views
introduced later. A body may instead be written \code{[\&](int k, int j, int i)},
receiving coordinates directly; when both forms are viable this three-argument form
is selected. The coordinate form exists mainly as a convenience for porting an
existing \code{par_for} body quickly --- it lets old code that indexes fields by
\code{(k, j, i)} drop in with minimal change and without adopting pack views or the
single-index conventions. It is not otherwise preferred: it does not compose with
the view/offset helpers, and because access no longer reduces to a flat
\code{var[idx]} it will generally inhibit vectorization. Reach for it to migrate a
loop; prefer \code{auto idx} for anything written fresh.

\paragraph{Lambda markings.} Mark the \code{outer} body with \code{KOKKOS_LAMBDA}
(it is captured and run on the device); leave the \code{inner} bodies as plain
\code{[\&]} lambdas --- they are defined \emph{inside} the outer device lambda, so
they are already device code and capture by reference. The outer body must name its
parameter type rather than use \code{auto}, because nvcc rejects generic extended
(\code{__host__ __device__}) lambdas. The \code{idx_space_t::idx_range_t} alias is
that type; spell it that way rather than reaching for \code{decltype} on the index
space object.

\paragraph{Prefer \code{auto} for everything else.} Practically, the single most
useful habit when writing these kernels is to declare almost every local with
\code{auto} rather than a concrete type. The types the abstraction hands you ---
the inner-loop index, the scratch object, the pack and flux views, the offset
returned by \code{GetDelta} --- all \emph{change with the tags}. The index passed
to an \code{[\&](auto idx)} body is a flat \code{int} under one inner tag and an
\code{Index3} under another; the scratch object is a stack buffer, a bump-arena
slice, or a team-scratch view depending on the loop tag and backend; \code{GetDelta}
returns a memory offset for the flat paths and a coordinate offset for the
coordinate path. Writing \code{auto qp = la::GetPerPointScratch<Real>(rp)} and
\code{[\&](auto idx)} lets the \emph{same kernel text} recompile under a different
tag with no edits; spelling a concrete type instead pins the kernel to one
structure and defeats the whole point. The one exception is the outer lambda's
\code{InnerIndexRange} parameter, which must be named for the nvcc reason given
above --- and the \code{idx_range_t} alias fills that role without pinning the
kernel to a tag.

The two subsections below detail the \code{IndexSpace} and the
\code{InnerIndexRange}. The section after them covers the three compile-time tags
that select the loop structure, and the later sections --- offsets, scratch,
halos, pack views --- add the remaining pieces onto this skeleton in roughly that
order.

% =====================================================================
\subsection{Index spaces and coordinates}
\label{sec:indexspace}
% =====================================================================

The \code{IndexSpace} is the object a kernel iterates over, and it is where the
compile-time structure choices are recorded. Its first three template parameters ---
\code{loop_tag}, \code{inner_tag}, and \code{loop_backend} --- are the subjects of
the next three sections (a fourth, \code{reduction}, defaults to a non-reducing space
and is deferred to Section~\ref{sec:reductions}); this section describes the index
information the object carries and the coordinate conventions every loop body relies
on.

\paragraph{Two indexers: logical and memory.} An \code{IndexSpace} holds two
descriptions of a block's index space:
\begin{itemize}
\item the \emph{logical} indexer (\code{GetLogicalIndexer()}), covering the
  interior $(k,j,i)$ cells the kernel is required to update; and
\item the \emph{memory} indexer (\code{GetMemoryIndexer()}), covering the full
  allocated extent of a block, ghost cells included.
\end{itemize}
Both are \code{Indexer3D} objects ordered $(k, j, i)$ from slowest- to
fastest-varying, matching the in-memory layout: $i$ is contiguous, then $j$, then
$k$. The logical indexer defines \emph{what must be touched}; the memory indexer
defines \emph{where things live} and how a $(k,j,i)$ triple maps to a flat memory
offset. Keeping the two separate is what lets the abstraction talk precisely about
cells that are visited but not ``owned'': a \code{memory}-tag traversal
(Section~\ref{sec:innertags}) may sweep ghost cells that fall inside a contiguous
run, and a halo (Section~\ref{sec:halos}) deliberately extends the visited set
past the logical interior. Both are expressed as excursions in the memory indexer
around the logical set.

\paragraph{Construction.} There are two constructors. The one used in production
takes a \code{MeshData} / \code{MeshBlockData} pointer and an
\code{IndexDomain}, and reads the block bounds and topological-element layout from
the mesh:
\begin{lstlisting}
IndexSpace<LOOP_TAG, INNER_TAG> idx_space(
    ninner,                      // inner chunk size (see Loop tags)
    IndexDomain::interior,       // which cells are "logical"
    halo,                        // uniform extra logical cells per active dir
    nblocks, md,                 // block count and mesh data
    TopologicalElement::F1,      // element the *logical* space is over (e.g. an x1 face)
    TopologicalElement::CC);     // element that fixes the *memory* extents (default CC)
\end{lstlisting}
This is the constructor that knows the true dimensionality of the problem: it
consults \code{md->GetMeshPointer()->ndim} and only widens the logical bounds by
\code{halo} in directions the mesh actually resolves.

\paragraph{The \code{halo} argument (an unfortunate name).} The integer \code{halo}
simply grows the logical index space outward by that many cells in every
non-degenerate direction: the interior it would otherwise cover is padded by
\code{halo} cells on each side in $i$ (and in $j$, $k$ where those directions are
resolved). It is how a kernel asks to iterate a few ghost cells as if they were
interior --- e.g.\ to fill a layer of boundary cells with the same loop that does
the interior. Note the unfortunate name clash: this run-time \code{halo} integer
has \emph{nothing to do} with the compile-time halo \emph{types}
(\code{halo::minus_i_t} and friends) passed to \code{AddHalo}
(Section~\ref{sec:halos}). The former uniformly enlarges the base iteration space;
the latter names specific shifted copies of an inner range for producer/consumer
reuse. They are entirely separate mechanisms that happen to share a word; pass
\code{halo = 0} whenever the base interior is what you want.

Note that the topological element appears \emph{twice}, and the two roles are
distinct. The first (\code{domain_te}, here \code{F1}) is the element the
\emph{logical} index space is over --- it is what makes this, e.g., a loop over
$x_1$ faces rather than cell centers. The second (\code{memory_te}, defaulting to
\code{CC}) fixes the \emph{memory} extents the block's storage is sized to, and
hence the strides all flat/memory indexing uses. Only two memory layouts are
allowed, \code{CC} (cell-centered) and \code{NN} (node-centered): a face- or
edge-centered field is stored in an array whose extents match one of these two
(a face field has an extra entry in its normal direction, like a nodal layout in
that direction), so the memory element is not free to be anything. Keeping
\code{memory_te} separate from \code{domain_te} is what lets a kernel iterate a
face-centered logical space while indexing into storage laid out for its actual
allocation.

\paragraph{Relation to a traditional \code{par_for} iteration space.} It is worth
seeing how this constructor lines up with the bounds a Parthenon kernel already
spells out by hand. A conventional multi-block \code{par_for} over the interior
looks like:
\begin{lstlisting}
IndexRange ib = md->GetBoundsI(IndexDomain::interior, TopologicalElement::F1);
IndexRange jb = md->GetBoundsJ(IndexDomain::interior, TopologicalElement::F1);
IndexRange kb = md->GetBoundsK(IndexDomain::interior, TopologicalElement::F1);
par_for(loop_pattern_mdrange_tag, "name", DevExecSpace(),
        0, nblocks - 1, kb.s, kb.e, jb.s, jb.e, ib.s, ib.e,
        KOKKOS_LAMBDA(const int b, const int k, const int j, const int i) { ... });
\end{lstlisting}
The \code{IndexSpace} constructor arguments are the same information, gathered into
one object: the \code{IndexDomain} and the block count play the roles of the
\code{GetBounds*} calls and the \code{0..nblocks-1} range, and the mesh pointer is
what those bounds are read from. What the \code{IndexSpace} adds are two arguments
with no analogue in the \code{par_for} call. The first is \code{ninner}: a
\code{par_for} does not chunk the $kji$ space, so it has nothing to size, whereas
the \code{IndexSpace} needs the chunk size to build its \code{outer}/\code{inner}
split (Section~\ref{sec:looptags}). The second is the memory topological element:
a \code{par_for} body indexes fields directly through their own views, so the
iteration space never needs to know the storage layout, but the \code{IndexSpace}
computes flat indices itself and so must be told the memory extents. Both are
inessential in the simplest case --- \code{ninner} has a sensible default shape and
\code{memory_te} defaults to \code{CC} --- so a cell-centered kernel's constructor
call is little more than the \code{par_for} bounds with the tags attached.

\paragraph{Why the block count is passed explicitly.} The constructor takes both a
\code{MeshData} pointer \emph{and} an \code{nblocks}, which looks redundant --- why
not read the block count from \code{md}? Because the two are not always the same.
A \code{SparsePack} may be built over a \emph{subset} of the blocks in a
\code{MeshData} (for instance, only the blocks on a given refinement level, or
those selected by some predicate), so the number of blocks a kernel actually
iterates is a property of the \emph{pack}, not of the \code{MeshData} it was drawn
from. Pulling the count from \code{md} would silently iterate blocks the pack does
not contain --- indexing past the pack's block dimension --- which has bitten us
more than once. The correct source is the pack: pass \code{pack.GetNBlocks()}. The
\code{md} pointer is still needed, but only for the bounds and dimensionality; it
does not define how many blocks the loop covers.

A second, simpler \code{IndexSpace} constructor takes explicit extents
\code{(nblocks, nx, ny, nz, nghost)} and is used by unit tests. It always pads
ghosts in every direction, so it describes a fully-dimensional block --- an extent
of $1$ denotes a \emph{thin active} direction (with ghosts), not a symmetry
direction. This should probably not be used in downstream code. 

\paragraph{Degenerate vs.\ thin-active directions, and \codelbl{GetNdim}.} A
\emph{degenerate} (symmetry) direction has interior extent $1$ \emph{and no ghost
cells} --- its memory extent is also $1$. A \emph{thin active} direction has
interior extent $1$ but carries ghosts, so its memory extent exceeds $1$.
Distinguishing the two is essential for halos: an offset into a degenerate
direction names a plane that does not exist and must be dropped, whereas an offset
into a thin active direction is legitimate (Section~\ref{sec:reduced}).
\code{GetNdim()} therefore counts a direction as active iff its \emph{memory}
extent exceeds $1$ --- ``is there a cell to shift into?'' --- which is the same
criterion \code{GetDelta} uses and is consistent across both constructors.

\paragraph{Coordinates handed to a body.} Within a loop body the current point is
recovered from whatever index form the inner tag produces via
\code{range.GetKJI(idx)}, which returns the $(k,j,i)$ triple regardless of whether
\code{idx} is a flat integer, an \code{Index3}, or a memory offset. Neighbor
access is expressed with offset objects from \code{GetDelta} rather than raw
integer arithmetic; Section~\ref{sec:getdelta} explains why the offset is an
object and how it interacts with degenerate directions.

% =====================================================================
\subsection{The inner range}
\label{sec:innerrange}
% =====================================================================

Where the \code{IndexSpace} describes a whole block's iteration space, the
\code{InnerIndexRange} --- the \code{r} handed to the outer body and passed to
\code{inner} --- describes \emph{one slice} of it. Exactly what a slice is depends
on the loop tag. For \code{bvoi}, \code{outer} runs over blocks only, so the range
represents an \emph{entire block}'s $kji$ space (the chunking into contiguous runs
happens later, inside \code{inner}). For \code{bovi}, \code{outer} runs over
(block, chunk) pairs, so the range represents \emph{one chunk} of a block. For
\code{boiv} the range is a single $(k,j,i)$ cell. In every case it is the running
state of the outer loop, and it is what every per-slice facility keys off of ---
\code{inner} iterates it, \code{GetPerPointScratch} sizes a buffer to it,
\code{AddHalo} extends it, and the pack views are constructed against it.

Its main job for a loop body is index translation. Whatever index form the inner
tag hands the body --- a flat integer, an \code{Index3}, or a memory offset ---
\code{r.GetKJI(idx)} converts it to the $(k,j,i)$ triple, and the range knows the
slice's origin in both the logical and memory indexers, so it can map between the
body's index, a memory-flat offset, and coordinates. This is why the same body
text works under different inner tags: the range absorbs the difference between
``a flat index into this slice'' and ``these $(k,j,i)$ coordinates.''

A body rarely constructs an \code{InnerIndexRange} itself --- \code{outer} builds
it and hands it in --- but it does name the type (to spell the outer lambda's
parameter, via \code{idx_space_t::idx_range_t}) and call methods on it
(\code{GetKJI}, \code{TeamBarrier}, \code{AddHalo}). The \code{bvoi} and \code{bovi}
forms carry the slice's extent on the range (a whole block and a single chunk
respectively); the pointwise \code{boiv} form carries a single $(k,j,i)$ point,
which is why its \code{inner} is a one-iteration ``loop''
(Section~\ref{sec:looptags}).

% =====================================================================
\section{Compile-time loop structure selection}
\label{sec:selection}
% =====================================================================

The three structure-selecting \code{IndexSpace} template parameters ---
\code{loop_tag}, \code{inner_tag}, and \code{loop_backend} --- are what turn one
kernel body into a specific loop structure at compile time. (The fourth parameter,
\code{reduction}, does not affect loop structure and is covered separately in
Section~\ref{sec:reductions}.) None of them changes what the body computes; together
they decide how the $(b,k,j,i)$ work is nested, how a chunk is walked, and which
backend emits the loops. They are independent axes, so any combination is a valid
\code{IndexSpace} (with the single exception noted under inner tags), and switching
structure is a matter of changing a tag on the \code{using} alias. The three
subsections below take them in turn.

% =====================================================================
\subsection{Loop tags: where the variable level sits}
\label{sec:looptags}
% =====================================================================

The abstraction organizes work into four conceptual levels: blocks ($b$), an
\code{outer} traversal, an \code{inner} traversal, and a ``variable'' ($v$) level.
The $v$ level is any per-block work the user writes between the block and the
innermost $kji$ traversal --- most often a loop over the fields or components a
kernel updates, but in general any block-local computation. The \code{loop_tag}
selects where that $v$ level sits in the
$\text{block}\to\text{outer}\to\text{inner}$ hierarchy, and that placement fixes
the generated loop structure.

\paragraph{The key axis: chunking and fusion.} Both hierarchical tags,
\code{bvoi} and \code{bovi}, \emph{chunk} the $kji$ space into runs of
\code{ninner} cells; only \code{boiv} does no chunking (each point stands alone).
Where the two hierarchical tags differ is \emph{which loop level} the chunk loop
lives at. In \code{bovi} the chunking is at the \code{outer} level: \code{outer}
runs over (block, chunk) pairs and each \code{inner} covers one chunk. In
\code{bvoi} the \code{outer} loop runs over blocks only, but the single
\code{inner} call still splits internally into a chunk loop and a within-chunk
loop --- so \code{bvoi} chunks too, just \emph{inside} \code{inner} rather than in
\code{outer}. In particular, a \code{bvoi} \code{memory} traversal walks the same
per-chunk contiguous spans, and sweeps the same in-span ghost cells, as a
\code{bovi} one; the difference between the two tags is where the user's variable
loop sits and how scratch is scoped (below), not whether chunking happens.

The chunk size is the \code{ninner} argument to the constructor, expressed either
as a raw cell count or as a \code{chunk_shape} (\code{i_pencil}, \code{ij_slab},
\code{kji_cube}) resolved against the indexer. This is the ``how much to fuse''
knob planted in Section~\ref{sec:cpu}: a larger chunk fuses more $kji$ work into
one contiguous \code{inner} sweep --- better for vectorization, at the cost of
more live scratch and, for \code{memory}-tag traversals, more swept ghost work.

\paragraph{What chunking looks like.} Because ``chunking the $kji$ space'' is not
obvious until you have drawn it, Figure~\ref{fig:chunk} works a small example on a
single $ji$ plane (the $k$ index held fixed; $kji$ chunking is the same idea with
one more dimension). The cells of a block are stored row-major --- $i$ contiguous,
then $j$, then $k$ --- and a chunk is a run of cells in that flat order. As long
as a chunk stays within a single interior row --- i.e.\ the chunk size is at most
the interior row length $n_i$ and divides it evenly, such as \code{i_pencil}
(panel a) --- each chunk is a clean pencil and the picture is unsurprising.

The moment a chunk \emph{straddles} a row boundary, the logical and \code{memory}
traversals diverge. This happens whenever a chunk cannot be contained in one row:
any \code{ninner} $> n_i$ straddles (a chunk of $2 n_i$ spans parts of three rows),
and so does any \code{ninner} $< n_i$ that does not divide $n_i$ evenly (panel b,
with $n_i = 6$ and \code{ninner}${}= 8$, is one such case, but so is
\code{ninner}${}= 2 n_i = 12$). What matters is only whether a chunk's cell run
crosses from the end of one interior row into the next --- not the specific
relation between \code{ninner} and $n_i$. When it does, the run is still well
defined in flat order, but it now contains a row boundary, and that is where the
two inner tags part ways.

The divergence is the point of panel~(c). A \code{logical} traversal
(\code{logical_flat} / \code{logical_coords}) visits only interior cells: at each
row boundary inside the chunk it jumps directly from the last interior cell of one
row to the first interior cell of the next, skipping the ghost cells at the ends of
the rows. A \code{memory} traversal instead walks the \emph{contiguous memory
span} from the chunk's first cell to its last, so the ghost cells that physically
lie between the rows in memory (the trailing ghost of the lower row and the
leading ghost of the upper row, at each crossed boundary) are swept as part of the
run. Both traversals touch every interior cell of the chunk exactly once; the
\code{memory} one additionally touches those in-span ghosts, which is the ``swept
ghost work'' the fusion knob trades against, and why a \code{chunk_shape} that
lands on clean row/plane boundaries (\code{i_pencil}, \code{ij_slab}) avoids the
extra work entirely.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\input{chunking_tikz.tex}
\caption{Chunking of a block's $kji$ space, drawn on one $ji$ plane. The heavy
outline is the interior; the light border is the one-cell ghost ring.
\textbf{(a)} \code{i_pencil}: each chunk is exactly one interior row.
\textbf{(b)} A chunk size ($n_{\mathrm{inner}}=8$, here with $n_i=6$) whose chunks
straddle row boundaries --- as any chunk not contained within a single interior
row does --- while a \emph{logical} traversal still touches only interior cells. \textbf{(c)} The same chunk~0 under a
\code{memory} traversal: the contiguous memory span from the chunk's first to last
cell also sweeps the two ghost cells (\textcolor{red!75!black}{g}) that lie between
the rows in memory. Interior cells are still each touched exactly once.}
\label{fig:chunk}
\end{figure}

\begin{description}
\item[\codelbl{bvoi} (block, var, outer, inner)] The $v$ loop sits \emph{above} the
  traversal, so one $v$ iteration spans the whole $kji$ space of a block.
  \code{outer} runs over blocks only. Halos and scratch are \emph{block}-relative
  (sized to the whole block). This is a mixed logical/memory path. It is a
  CPU-friendly hierarchical form and in practice often the fastest, at the price
  of the most scratch --- the entire block's worth of intermediates is live at
  once.
\item[\codelbl{bovi} (block, outer, var, inner)] The $v$ loop sits \emph{inside} the
  chunk \code{outer} loop but \emph{around} the \code{inner} traversal, so each
  $v$ iteration covers one $kji$ chunk. \code{outer} runs over (block, chunk)
  pairs. Halos and scratch are \emph{chunk}-relative. This is the main
  contiguous-span path and the other CPU-friendly form (\S\ref{sec:cpu}); it needs
  far less scratch than \code{bvoi} because only one chunk is live, at the cost of
  re-establishing per-chunk state more often.
\item[\codelbl{boiv} (block, outer, inner, var)] The $v$ loop is innermost, at a
  single logical cell, so the \code{inner} ``traversal'' is one point --- the
  degenerate \code{ninner}${=}1$ limit of \code{bovi}, given its own code path for
  performance. \code{outer} is a flat grid over all $(\text{block}, k, j, i)$.
  This is the pointwise GPU-friendly form (\S\ref{sec:gpu}); scratch is a tiny
  per-thread stack buffer and there is no producer/consumer barrier.
\end{description}

Where $v$ sits also governs what state is in scope for the user's block-local
loop and how scratch and halos are sized (block-relative for \code{bvoi},
chunk-relative for \code{bovi}/\code{boiv}), which is why the same halo annotation
produces different amounts of scratch under different tags.

% =====================================================================
\subsection{Inner tags: how a chunk is traversed}
\label{sec:innertags}
% =====================================================================

Where the \code{loop_tag} decides how the $kji$ space is split across
\code{outer}/\code{inner}, the \code{inner_tag} decides how a single \code{inner}
chunk is walked and what index form the body receives. All three variants satisfy
the same coverage contract --- every logical cell is touched exactly once --- but
they differ in what the body is handed and which cells beyond the logical set may
also be visited.

\begin{description}
\item[\codelbl{logical\_flat}] Visits each logical cell exactly once and hands the body
  a single flat integer suitable for direct \code{var[idx]} pointer access. This
  requires that every field touched in the kernel share one memory layout, so they
  can share the flat index. It is the most likely to vectorize, because the inner
  loop reduces to \code{a[idx]}-style accesses over a contiguous run --- this is
  the tag behind the CPU form of \S\ref{sec:cpu}.
\item[\codelbl{logical\_coords}] Same exactly-once logical coverage, but the body
  receives an \code{Index3} of $(k,j,i)$ instead of a flat integer. Use this when
  the fields in a kernel do \emph{not} share a layout --- for instance a
  cell-centered field and a face-centered field accessed together --- so no single
  flat index is valid for all of them. Access goes through coordinates, which is
  also the natural form for the pointwise \code{boiv} path.
\item[\codelbl{memory}] Walks the \emph{contiguous memory span} of the chunk rather
  than only its logical cells. It still touches every logical cell exactly once,
  but ghost cells that happen to fall inside the span are also visited (their
  post-loop values are not meaningful and must not be relied on). This can be the
  fastest option when it lets the loop consume one long uniform run of memory
  without gaps, trading a little wasted work on ghosts for a simpler, longer inner
  loop.
\end{description}

The \code{boiv}${+}$\code{memory} combination is rejected at compile time by a
\code{static_assert} in \code{IndexSpace}: a single-cell inner range has no
meaningful contiguous-span traversal, so the pairing is not a coherent contract.

% =====================================================================
\subsection{Backends}
\label{sec:backends}
% =====================================================================

The third \code{IndexSpace} template parameter selects the backend that actually
emits the loops:
\begin{itemize}
\item \code{loop_backend::raw} --- a plain host loop nest with \code{#pragma omp
  simd} on the innermost loop. This is the form that gives the C++ compiler the
  cleanest view for auto-vectorization on a CPU.
\item \code{loop_backend::kokkos} --- dispatch through Kokkos parallel policies
  (a flat \code{RangePolicy} for \code{boiv}, a \code{TeamPolicy} with team/vector
  ranges for the hierarchical tags).
\end{itemize}
It defaults to \code{default_loop_backend_v}, which is \code{raw} when the device
execution space \emph{is} the host space (a CPU-only build) and \code{kokkos}
otherwise. \code{outer} and \code{inner} choose between the two with
\code{if constexpr} on \code{backend_v}, so the unused path is never instantiated
and the selection carries no runtime cost.

The backend is orthogonal to the loop and inner tags: any tag combination can be
compiled through either backend, and the two are required to produce the same
logical result for a given \code{IndexSpace} (Section~\ref{sec:contracts}).
Pinning the backend explicitly, rather than taking the default, is mainly useful
in tests that want to exercise a specific backend regardless of the build --- for
example, to check raw-vs-Kokkos parity on a host build where both can run.

% =====================================================================
\section{Offsets and neighbor access}
\label{sec:getdelta}
% =====================================================================

Many kernels read a cell's neighbors, whether or not scratch or halos are
involved: a diffusion operator reads $q(f\pm\hat{\imath})$, a gradient reads
neighbors in every direction, the flux consumer reads a produced state one cell
over. To do this the body needs a way to spell ``one cell over in $x_1$'' that is
correct for whatever index form the current inner tag hands it. \code{GetDelta}
provides exactly that, and it is a general facility --- nothing about it is tied to
scratch or halos.

\code{idx_space.GetDelta(dir)} returns an \emph{offset object} representing a
$+1$ logical step in direction \code{dir}, usable directly in the body's index
arithmetic:
\begin{lstlisting}
const auto dx1 = idx_space.GetDelta(X1DIR);   // one +x1 step

la::inner(r, [&](auto f) {
  // a plain three-point Laplacian in x1 -- no scratch, no halo:
  out(f) = q(f - dx1) - 2.0 * q(f) + q(f + dx1);
});
\end{lstlisting}
The step is \emph{signed through the coefficient}: \code{f + dx1} moves in
$+\hat{\imath}$, \code{f - dx1} in $-\hat{\imath}$, \code{f + 2*dx1} two cells up.
The delta always represents the positive unit step; the direction of travel is in
how the body combines it.

\paragraph{Why an object, not an integer.} \code{GetDelta} does not return a plain
\code{int}. For the flat-indexed paths a $+1$ step in $x_1$ is a $+1$ change in the
flat index, but a $+1$ step in $x_2$ is a jump of one full $i$-row, and in $x_3$ a
jump of one full $ij$-plane --- the numeric stride depends on the block's memory
extents. The returned object carries the correct memory stride \emph{and} the
$(dk,dj,di)$ logical components, and it overloads \code{+}/\code{-}/scalar-multiply
so that adding it to a flat index, an \code{Index3}, or a memory offset each does
the right thing. That is what lets \code{f - dx1} appear unchanged in a kernel that
might be compiled with \code{logical_flat}, \code{logical_coords}, or a
\code{memory} tag: the same expression resolves to flat-index arithmetic or
coordinate arithmetic as appropriate.

\paragraph{Degenerate directions collapse to zero.} \code{GetDelta} returns a
\emph{zero} step for a direction that is not resolved: \code{GetDelta(X3DIR)} in a
2D problem has all components zero, so \code{f + dx3} is just \code{f}. A neighbor
read in an unresolved direction therefore reads the cell itself --- the right
behavior for a symmetry direction, and it makes a stencil kernel written for 3D
also correct, unchanged, in 2D. This zeroing is keyed off the same memory-extent
test as \code{GetNdim} (Section~\ref{sec:indexspace}), and it is the consumer-side
counterpart of the producer-side halo behavior in
Section~\ref{sec:reduced}: a consumer's \code{f - dx3} and a producer's $-\hat{k}$
halo offset agree about which directions are real.

% =====================================================================
\section{Scratch}
\label{sec:scratch}
% =====================================================================

Scratch is the per-cell temporary storage a producer inner loop writes and a
consumer inner loop reads --- the reconstructed $q^\pm$ of the flux kernel. It is
the mechanism that makes the store-once CPU form possible, and it is sized
and allocated differently for each loop path so that the same kernel text works on
all of them.

\paragraph{Registration and sizing (two-phase).} Scratch is declared in two steps.
At setup, before any kernel launch, the amount of scratch per point is
\emph{registered} on the \code{IndexSpace}:
\begin{lstlisting}
idx_space.AddPerPointScratch<Real>();          // one Real per point
idx_space.AddPerPointScratch<Real, 2, 3>();    // a 2x3 block per point
idx_space.AddPerPointScratch<Real, recon_halo>(2); // 2 Reals/point, sized for the halo range
\end{lstlisting}
The template arguments after the type are optional trailing extents for a
\emph{shaped} per-point block (so \code{scratch(c0, c1, idx)} indexes a small dense
array at each cell), and an optional \code{Halo} type that tells the sizing to
account for the halo-extended range a producer will cover
(Section~\ref{sec:halos}). Registration accumulates a per-team scratch budget;
for the Kokkos backend this is what is requested from the team-scratch pool when
the kernel launches. Then, inside the outer body, the actual buffer is obtained:
\begin{lstlisting}
auto scratch = la::GetPerPointScratch<Real>(range);   // range may be a halo-extended range
\end{lstlisting}
Passing a halo-extended range (\S\ref{sec:halos}) yields a buffer that covers the
extended point set, so a producer can write every point a consumer will later
read.

\paragraph{Uniform indexing, backend-specialized storage.} The returned object
accepts the same index forms the loop body uses --- \code{scratch(idx)} for a flat
index, \code{scratch(Index3\{k,j,i\})} or \code{scratch(k,j,i)} for coordinates,
and \code{scratch(c0, c1, idx)} for a shaped block --- but the storage underneath
is chosen per loop path and backend:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{\code{boiv}}: a tiny fixed-size \emph{stack} buffer, just large
  enough for the point and its halo neighbors (a handful of cells). Nothing is
  shared between threads, matching the recompute-per-cell GPU form.
\item \textbf{raw backend (hierarchical tags)}: a non-owning slice of a per-thread
  \emph{bump arena} --- a region allocator that hands out memory by advancing a
  pointer and reclaims everything at once when the next outer iteration resets it
  (\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region-based_memory_management}{region-based
  allocation}). This gives cheap, allocation-free scratch reuse across chunks on
  the CPU.
\item \textbf{Kokkos backend (hierarchical tags)}: Kokkos team scratch, i.e.\
  GPU shared memory (or its host equivalent), shared across the threads of a team.
\end{itemize}
Because the indexing API is uniform, the kernel body never names which of these it
got.

\paragraph{\code{bvoi} vs \code{bovi}: block-relative vs chunk-relative scratch.}
The two hierarchical tags use the same storage backends but differ in \emph{how
much} scratch is live and \emph{for how long}, because their scratch is scoped to
different index sets (Section~\ref{sec:looptags}). In \code{bovi}, \code{outer}
runs over (block, chunk) pairs and the outer body sees one chunk at a time, so
\code{GetPerPointScratch} is obtained \emph{per chunk} and sized to a single chunk
(times any halo extension). Only that one chunk's worth of scratch is live at once;
the buffer is re-established as the chunk loop advances, and it need only be large
enough for one chunk. In \code{bvoi}, \code{outer} runs over blocks only and the
single \code{inner} call covers the whole block, so scratch is \emph{block}-relative
--- sized to the entire block (times halo) and live for the block's whole
traversal. A producer/consumer pair in \code{bvoi} can therefore reconstruct once
into a block-sized buffer and consume anywhere in the block, whereas in \code{bovi}
the reuse is confined to a chunk.

The practical trade-off follows directly. \code{bvoi} needs substantially more
scratch --- on the Kokkos backend, enough team/shared memory to hold a full block's
intermediates, which can limit occupancy or exceed the scratch budget for large
blocks or many scratch variables --- but it establishes scratch and pays
per-chunk setup only once per block, and it never has to worry about a
producer/consumer dependency crossing a chunk boundary. \code{bovi} keeps the
scratch footprint small and fixed (one chunk), which is friendlier to shared-memory
limits, at the cost of re-establishing per-chunk state on every chunk and confining
any scratch reuse to within a chunk. The chunk size (\code{ninner}) is the knob
that trades between them: larger chunks approach \code{bvoi}'s footprint and reuse,
smaller chunks minimize live scratch. This is why the flux kernel's choice between
\code{bvoi} and \code{bovi} (Section~\ref{sec:onekernel}) is really a choice about
scratch scope, not about what the kernel computes.

\paragraph{Barriers.} When a consumer reads scratch that \emph{other} threads
wrote --- the hierarchical tags, where a team cooperates on a chunk --- the
producer loop must complete before the consumer starts. Call
\code{range.TeamBarrier()} between the two inner loops, exactly as with raw
hierarchical Kokkos parallelism. On the \code{boiv} path the producer and consumer
run back-to-back on the same thread and share no scratch, so \code{TeamBarrier} is
a no-op; writing it anyway is what keeps the one kernel correct under every tag.

\paragraph{A per-cell reduction over a variable loop.} Scratch is not only for the
reconstruction/flux coupling. A common use is to accumulate a per-cell quantity
across a variable loop and then reuse it --- for instance, summing the sparse
components of a pack variable \code{var} into a per-cell total that several later
terms read. Compute it once into scratch, with the component loop sitting
\emph{outside} the inner cell loop:
\begin{lstlisting}
idx_space.AddPerPointScratch<Real>();          // one Real per cell

la::outer(idx_space, KOKKOS_LAMBDA(const idx_space_t::idx_range_t &r, int b) {
  auto total = la::GetPerPointScratch<Real>(r);
  auto pv = la::make_pack_view(r, pack);

  total.Zero();
  r.TeamBarrier();

  // Component loop OUTSIDE the inner cell loop. The component count comes from the
  // pack for this block (a sparse variable's extent can vary block to block); var(c)
  // is the tag for the c-th component of var.
  const int nc = pack.GetSize(b, var());
  for (int c = 0; c < nc; ++c) {
    la::inner(r, [&](auto idx) { total(idx) += pv(var(c), idx); });
    r.TeamBarrier();   // finish this component's contribution before the next
  }

  // Reuse the accumulated total.
  la::inner(r, [&](auto idx) {
    out(idx) = something(total(idx), ...);
  });
});
\end{lstlisting}
The structure to notice is that the variable (component) loop is the outer of the
two loop levels: each iteration is a full inner sweep that adds one component's
contribution into the running per-cell total, with a barrier between iterations so
that a cell's accumulator is complete before the next sweep touches it. A loop of
exactly this shape --- a user-written loop over variables wrapped around the inner
$kji$ traversal --- is the origin of the $v$ (``variable'') level name in the loop
hierarchy (Section~\ref{sec:looptags}). Taking the component count from the pack
per block, rather than from a global constant, is what lets this work when a sparse
variable's extent varies from block to block. The final inner loop reads back the
finished total. This is per-cell scratch used
purely as an accumulator --- a very common pattern, and one that uses only the
registration, \code{GetPerPointScratch}, and barrier machinery above.

% =====================================================================
\subsection{Type-indexed scratch}
\label{sec:typed-scratch}
% =====================================================================

The scratch shown so far is indexed by a bare component number:
\code{scratch(c0, c1, idx)} for a shaped block, or \code{scratch(m, idx)} if you
lay materials out along a component axis by hand. That works, but it forces the
kernel to remember which component number means which quantity, and to keep the
sizing (\code{AddPerPointScratch<Real, N>}) in sync with that hand-rolled layout.
\emph{Type-indexed scratch} removes that bookkeeping: you describe the scratch
contents as a list of variable \emph{types} once, and then index the buffer by
type --- the same field tags used with the pack views --- rather than by number.

\begin{lstlisting}
// A compile-time list of the variable types this scratch holds.
using recon_vars = la::IndexedVarTypeList<rho, vel, pres>;

// Register / obtain scratch sized to hold those types (optionally x NMAT materials,
// optionally over a halo range).
la::AddTypeIndexedPerPointScratch<Real, recon_vars, /*NMAT=*/nmat>(idx_space);
...
auto sc = la::GetTypeIndexedPerPointScratch<Real, recon_vars, nmat>(rp);

// Index by variable type (and component, material, topological element as needed):
sc(rho(),  idx)          = ...;   // scalar
sc(vel(1), idx)          = ...;   // component 1 of a vector variable
sc(rho(),  m, idx)       = ...;   // material m
sc(TE::F1, vel(0), idx)  = ...;   // an x1-face-centered component
\end{lstlisting}

The \code{IndexedVarTypeList} fixes a layout: each variable occupies a contiguous
block of components sized by its own \code{size()}, laid out in the order listed,
and (when a material count is given) that whole per-cell record is replicated per
material. \code{GetTypeIndexedPerPointScratch} allocates a flat buffer of exactly
that size and hands back a wrapper whose \code{operator()} maps a
\code{(field_tag, ..., idx)} argument list to the right flat slot. This is not
fundamental ---
it compiles down to the same flat scratch access as the numeric form --- but in a
kernel that reconstructs a dozen variables across several materials it is a large
practical convenience, and it keeps the scratch layout and the field identities in
one place instead of two. The sizing call and the access use the same
\code{recon_vars} list, so the buffer cannot fall out of sync with how it is
indexed.

\section{Halos}
\label{sec:halos}
% =====================================================================

Recall the pattern that motivated the abstraction (Section~\ref{sec:cpu}): a
\emph{producer} inner loop reconstructs each cell's states and writes them into
scratch, and a later \emph{consumer} inner loop reads that scratch back, but at
\emph{offset} positions --- the flux at face $f$ reads the stored states of cell
$f$ \emph{and} of cell $f - \hat{\imath}$. This offset read is the whole source of
the problem a halo solves. If both loops ran over the same set of cells $\Sset$, the
consumer's read at $f - \hat{\imath}$ would fall on a scratch cell the producer
never wrote for the boundary cells of $\Sset$. The producer must therefore fill
scratch not just on $\Sset$ but on $\Sset$ \emph{plus the shifted copies the
consumer will reach into}. A halo is how the kernel declares exactly which shifted
copies those are.

It is worth separating two things that are easy to run together:
\begin{description}
\item[The halo \emph{type}] (e.g.\ \code{halo::minus_i_t}) is a compile-time set of
  logical offsets. It encodes the \emph{access pattern the consumer will use on
  scratch} --- the little stencil of offset reads like ``$f$ and $f -
  \hat{\imath}$''. It is a fixed property of the kernel, known before any loop
  runs, and it is what you pass to \code{AddHalo<...>} and to
  \code{AddPerPointScratch<T, ...>}.
\item[The halo \emph{range}] is what that type produces when applied to a concrete
  inner range: the actual set of logical cells the producer must visit, namely the
  union of $\Sset$ with each of its shifted copies. This is a run-time set of
  points (one per inner slice), and it is what the producer's \code{inner} loop
  actually iterates.
\end{description}
The type is the \emph{intent} (``the consumer reads these neighbors''); the range
is the \emph{consequence} (``so the producer must cover these cells''). Formally,
for a halo type with offsets $\{h_1, h_2, \dots\}$ (the identity $\mathbf{0}$ is
always implicitly included, since the consumer always reads its own cell),
applying it to $\Sset$ gives the range
\[
  \texttt{AddHalo}\langle h_1, h_2, \dots\rangle(\Sset)
  \;=\; \Sset \,\cup\, \{p + h_1 : p \in \Sset\} \,\cup\,
  \{p + h_2 : p \in \Sset\} \,\cup\, \dots
\]
In the flux kernel the consumer reads \code{qp(f - dx1)}, one cell in $-i$, so the
type is \code{halo::minus_i_t} (offsets $\{\mathbf{0}, -\hat{\imath}\}$) and the
range it produces is $\Sset \cup \{p - \hat{\imath} : p \in \Sset\}$ --- the base
cells plus one extra cell below the bottom of the slice. That extra cell is
precisely the scratch entry the boundary consumer would otherwise read uninitialized.

Figure~\ref{fig:halo} draws this: the type is a fixed offset stencil (the insets),
and the range is the union of the inner range with its shifted copy (the crosses).
The panels vary the offset direction and the relationship between that direction
and the chunk layout, to show how the same type produces differently-shaped ranges.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{A halo is not a reconstruction stencil}
\label{sec:halo-not-stencil}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

The halo type is a stencil of sorts --- but of the \emph{consumer's scratch
reads}, not of the producer's field reads, and conflating the two is the single
most common point of confusion. Two stencils are in play, and they are unrelated
in size:
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{reconstruction stencil} is the set of \emph{input field} cells read
  to compute \emph{one} cell's reconstructed states. For a three-point reconstruction
  it is \code{q(f-dx1)}, \code{q(f)}, \code{q(f+dx1)}; a higher-order scheme reads a
  wider set. It lives entirely inside the producer body (the field arguments to
  \code{reconstruct}) and the abstraction never sees it.
\item The \emph{halo} is the set of offsets at which the \emph{consumer} reads the
  \emph{produced scratch}. In the flux kernel that is just $\{\mathbf{0},
  -\hat{\imath}\}$ --- a face reads the stored states of its own cell and of the
  cell one step in $-i$ --- regardless of how wide the reconstruction stencil was.
\end{itemize}
So a wide reconstruction stencil with a one-cell halo is the common, expected case:
reconstruction may read five input cells to produce each cell's scratch states, but
the flux loop still only reaches one produced cell away. The halo describes the
producer/consumer \emph{coupling} through scratch, and is what determines the
producer's iteration range; the reconstruction stencil describes the producer's own
inputs and does not.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Halos in reduced dimensionality}
\label{sec:reduced}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

A halo offset that points into a direction the problem does not resolve names a
shifted copy of the inner range that does not exist. A $k$-directed halo in a 2D
($x_1$--$x_2$) problem is the canonical case: there is no $k\pm1$ plane to shift
into. The abstraction must not let such an offset extend the index space, size
scratch, or drive a loop past the real cells.

\paragraph{DROP.} In a run where some direction is degenerate, the abstraction
keeps only the offsets whose components in every degenerate direction are zero,
and discards the rest. Because the halo offsets are stored sorted
lexicographically by $(dk, dj, di)$ and the degenerate directions are always the
most-significant sort keys ($k$, then $j$, in the Parthenon convention where
degenerate directions are the higher ones), the kept offsets form a single
contiguous run $[\text{begin}, \text{end})$. The identity is always kept. This
contiguity is what makes the reduction cheap: the span-merge and pointwise
enumeration that build a halo range simply iterate the sub-range instead of the
full offset list, and the sortedness they rely on is preserved.

\paragraph{PROJECT, and why DROP is usually enough.} The semantically correct
operation is not DROP but \emph{PROJECT}: replace each offset by its projection
onto the active directions (zero the degenerate components). Under the assumption
that a removed dimension is a genuine \emph{translational symmetry} direction ---
every field is constant along it --- PROJECT is exact. If the consumer reads a
produced value at $p+h$, symmetry makes that value equal to the one at the
projected point $\text{proj}(p+h)$, so producing the projected copy delivers
precisely what the consumer needs, and the reduced-dimension run agrees with the
fully-resolved run.

DROP and PROJECT agree exactly when the halo is \emph{closed under projection} ---
when every offset's projection is itself already a declared offset. This holds for
every physical stencil we expect: dense corner boxes (e.g.\ the $2\times2$ face-flux
corners of constrained-transport EMFs), single-direction extensions, and standard
3/5/7-point stencils. It fails only for sparse, L-shaped, or bare-diagonal halos ---
an offset like $(-1,0,2)$ present without its projection $(0,0,2)$ --- which do not
arise in the schemes this abstraction targets.

\paragraph{The compile-time guard.} Because DROP silently under-produces when the
halo is \emph{not} projection-closed, \code{AddHalo} enforces closure with a
\code{static_assert}. The check is dimension-independent: it tests closure under
both degenerations Parthenon can produce (2D, with $k$ inactive; and 1D, with $k$
and $j$ inactive), so a halo that passes is safe in any run regardless of runtime
dimensionality. A halo that fails is rejected at compile time with a message
directing the author to the fix: add the missing projection point. Doing so is
free of side effects --- it produces one extra scratch value that, by projection
closure, no consumer distinguishes from a value it already needed --- and it makes
DROP coincide with PROJECT for that halo. Implementing PROJECT proper (which
requires de-duplicating the offsets that collapse onto the same projected point) is
a deliberately deferred extension; until a genuinely non-closed halo is needed in
practice, the guard plus the one-line workaround covers every case.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implementation details}
\label{sec:halo-impl}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

This subsection sketches how a halo range is actually constructed. It is not needed
to \emph{use} halos, but it explains why the range is built the way it is and where
the sorted-offset requirement comes from. The general procedure is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Start from the base logical domain $D$ of the block (or slice).
\item From the halo offsets, form an \emph{extended} logical domain $D_h$ large
  enough to contain the base set $\Sset$ \emph{and} every shifted copy
  $\mathrm{shift}(\Sset, h)$. (This is what \code{AddHaloToIndexer} does: it grows
  the indexer bounds by the per-direction min/max of the kept offsets ---
  Section~\ref{sec:reduced} --- so no shifted copy falls outside $D_h$.)
\item Flatten $\Sset$ in $D_h$, giving one contiguous flat span.
\item Flatten each shifted copy $\mathrm{shift}(\Sset, h_n)$ in $D_h$, each also a
  contiguous span.
\item Merge spans that overlap or touch, leaving a compact union of disjoint spans.
\end{enumerate}
Flattening in the \emph{extended} domain $D_h$ (not the base $D$) is what makes each
shifted copy come out contiguous and correctly placed; the geometric shift is
resolved before any flattening, so no row-wrap can occur.

\paragraph{Why offsets must be sorted, and the single-pass merge.} Because the
number of offsets is small and fixed at compile time, the span union is held in a
small fixed-capacity list rather than allocated. The merge is a single linear pass:
each candidate span is compared only against the last emitted span, extending it if
they touch or overlap and starting a new span otherwise. That one-pass merge is
correct only if the spans arrive in increasing flat order, which in turn requires
the halo offsets to be listed sorted by increasing flat offset in $D_h$ ---
lexicographically by $(dk, dj, di)$. This is the contract the halo types must
satisfy (the \code{static_assert} on strict sorted order with a unique identity),
and it is also what makes the reduced-dimension range a contiguous sub-run
(Section~\ref{sec:reduced}): dropping the leading/trailing offsets of a sorted list
leaves a sorted list.

\paragraph{Scratch spans a single enclosing memory range.} The scratch buffer for a
halo range is sized not to the compact span union but to a single contiguous
\emph{memory}-flat span, so that indexing is one subtraction: a flat-index body's
index is already relative to the slice's memory origin, giving
\code{idx - scratch_index_start}, and a coordinate body maps $(k,j,i)$ through the
memory indexer and subtracts the same cached span start. Which span depends on the
loop tag, because the two hierarchical tags visit different cells. A \code{bvoi}
inner loop enumerates contiguous flat spans and converts each flat index back to
$(k,j,i)$, so it sweeps the whole rectangular halo-extended \emph{box} --- including
the multi-axis corner cells that lie in no single shifted copy (the low corner of a
7-point stencil with an extra $-2\hat{\imath}$ offset, say) --- and its scratch must therefore be
sized to that box, the memory-flat interval from the box's low corner to its high
corner, regardless of inner tag. (Sizing \code{bvoi} scratch to the shifted-copy
union instead under-allocates and produces a negative scratch index at those
corners.) A \code{bovi} inner loop visits only the union of shifted copies, so its
scratch is sized to the enclosing memory-flat interval of that union --- a tighter
span than the box, still slightly over-allocated where disjoint spans leave gaps.
The logical coverage \code{size()} of the range is cached at construction rather
than recomputed by re-walking the spans.

\paragraph{The same range, two backend realizations.} These mechanics are what let
the two backends realize the identical halo semantics differently
(Section~\ref{sec:scratch}). In a hierarchical (\code{bvoi}/\code{bovi}) loop the
base set $\Sset$ is many cells, the halo range is the merged union above, and
scratch is a team/host buffer over the enclosing memory span --- each cell is
reconstructed once and its stored states serve the flux solves on both of its
faces. In the pointwise \code{boiv} loop $\Sset$ is a single cell, so a one-offset
halo range is just $\{p, p+h\}$ and scratch collapses to a tiny per-thread stack
array (a few cells); a cell's reconstruction is simply redone in each adjacent
iteration that needs it.

\section{Pack, variable, and flux views}
\label{sec:views}
% =====================================================================

A kernel still has to read and write actual fields, and a \code{SparsePack} is
Parthenon's usual way to hold them. \emph{Views} are the adapters that let a pack
be accessed with the same index forms the loop body already uses --- a flat
\code{int}, an \code{Index3}, or explicit $k,j,i$ --- so the variable access
matches the loop contract instead of being spelled separately. They are the
first-class way to touch variables inside a loop-abstraction kernel.

\paragraph{The view constructors.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \code{make_pack_view(range, pack)} --- a view over all non-sparse variables
  in the pack.
\item \code{make_sparse_pack_view(range, pack, sparse_idx)} --- a view over the
  sparse variables at a given sparse index.
\item \code{make_var_view(range, pack, var)} --- a single-variable view.
\item \code{make_flux_pack_view(range, pack, dir)} / \code{make_flux_view(...)}
  --- the flux-array counterparts for one sweep direction.
\end{itemize}
Each accepts the same index forms the body produces, so a kernel can be written
once and reused across inner tags: \code{pv(var(), idx)},
\code{pv(var(), Index3\{k,j,i\})}, or \code{pv(TE::F1, var(), idx)} for a
particular topological element.

\paragraph{Pointer pull-out is where the view earns its keep.} As noted in
Section~\ref{sec:onekernel}, a view is not merely cosmetic. For
\code{logical_coords} (and the pointwise \code{boiv} path) it is a thin wrapper
that forwards each access straight through to the sparse pack --- the right choice
when fields have different layouts or when each thread handles one cell. For the
flat-indexed paths, however, the view \emph{pulls raw pointers out of the pack at
construction}, so an access like \code{pv(var(), f)} compiles to an
\code{a[idx]}-style load/store the compiler can vectorize, with none of the pack's
per-access indexing in the inner loop. This is a large part of why the CPU form of
\S\ref{sec:cpu} is fast, and the kernel gets it without spelling any pointer
arithmetic itself.

\paragraph{Flux views are deliberately narrow.} A flux view differs from an
ordinary Parthenon pack on purpose: it contains \emph{only} fluxes, and \emph{only}
for the single direction requested at construction. Ordinary packs let you request
fluxes in any direction from the same object; here, because a directional sweep
only ever touches one flux direction, the view is specialized to that direction so
the access is as direct as a variable access. A kernel that sweeps multiple
directions constructs one flux view per direction.

\section{Reductions}
\label{sec:reductions}
% =====================================================================

So far every loop path has \emph{written} a result at each cell it visits. A
reduction instead \emph{folds} a per-cell quantity down to a single value --- the
maximum wave speed over a block for a timestep constraint, the total mass in a
region, the dot product behind a solver residual. The quantity being combined is
still defined cell by cell, over exactly the logical-cell set the loop already
describes; a reduction just adds a rule for how those per-cell contributions merge
into one. That combining rule is a \emph{reducer} (\code{Kokkos::Sum<Real>},
\code{Kokkos::Max<Real>}, \dots), and the promise of the abstraction is the same as
for the plain loops: the same kernel text expresses the reduction under any loop
tag, and every logical cell contributes exactly once.

\code{inner\_reduce} is the reducing counterpart of \code{inner}. Where an
\code{inner} body writes ---
\begin{lstlisting}
la::inner(range, [&](auto idx) { out(idx) = f(idx); });
\end{lstlisting}
an \code{inner\_reduce} body is handed an accumulator to fold into, and the reducer
supplies the merge:
\begin{lstlisting}
la::inner_reduce(range, [&](auto idx, auto &v) { v = Kokkos::max(v, speed(idx)); });
\end{lstlisting}
The reduced value the kernel ultimately produces is a property of the
\emph{traversal}, not of any one \code{inner\_reduce} call, so --- like the loop and
inner tags --- the reducer rides on the index space rather than being restated at
each use. A \emph{reduction space} carries it:
\begin{lstlisting}
using rist = la::ReductionIndexSpace<lt, it, Kokkos::Sum<Real>>;
rist idx_space(/* ... */);

auto result = la::outer_reduce(idx_space,
  KOKKOS_LAMBDA(const rist::idx_range_t &r, int b) {
    // A plain inner() still does ordinary per-cell work here -- e.g. fill scratch.
    la::inner(r, [&](auto idx) { total(idx) = accumulate(...); });
    r.TeamBarrier();
    // inner_reduce folds into the reduction the space defines.
    la::inner_reduce(r, [&](auto idx, auto &v) { v += total(idx); });
  });
\end{lstlisting}
A region may mix several \code{inner\_reduce} calls with plain \code{inner} calls
(the reconstruct-then-reduce shape above), and they all fold into the one reduction
the space names.

Crucially, adding a reduction does not change the loop structure the tags select. A
reduction kernel is instantiated with the \emph{same} block/outer/inner hierarchy as
the plain-loop kernel with the same tags: \code{bvoi} still runs one team per block
with the variable loop above a whole-block inner traversal, \code{bovi} still tiles
the block into \code{ninner}-sized chunks with a team per chunk, and \code{boiv}
still walks one logical cell at a time. The fold simply rides along that traversal ---
each level of the hierarchy the tag defines accumulates its own partial, and the
partials combine up the hierarchy to the single result --- so the reduction visits
exactly the cells the plain loop would, in the same order of nesting, with the sole
exception that \code{memory} collapses to \code{logical\_flat} (discussed next). A
kernel can therefore be re-tagged for a different architecture without the reduction
needing any change, exactly as a plain kernel can.

This lockstep is not free-standing; it rests on how Kokkos expresses reductions. The
abstraction realizes each hierarchy level's partial with the matching Kokkos
construct --- a team-level \code{parallel\_reduce} for the inner traversal of the
team-based tags, a plain range reduction for \code{boiv} --- and relies on Kokkos to
combine those partials with the reducer's associative-commutative merge. The merge
being order-independent is what lets the same reducer sit under any tag and still give
one well-defined answer; a reduction whose combine truly depended on visitation order
would not be portable across the loop shapes, and is outside what this supports.

\paragraph{A reduction is defined only over logical cells.} This is what makes the
result well defined: a cell that is not part of the logical set has no meaningful
contribution, and counting one twice would corrupt the fold. Two consequences follow
directly. A reduction may not run over a halo-extended range --- a halo deliberately
revisits shifted copies of cells, which is exactly the double-counting a fold cannot
tolerate --- so \code{inner\_reduce} rejects halo ranges at compile time; extend a
range only for the producer \code{inner} loops that fill scratch, and reduce over the
base range. And the \code{memory} inner tag, whose contiguous spans may sweep ghost
cells, is treated as \code{logical\_flat} inside a reduction, visiting logical cells
only. Both are the same principle --- fold each logical cell once, and nothing else ---
rather than two separate rules. Note that this narrowing applies only to
\code{inner\_reduce}: a plain \code{inner} inside an \code{outer\_reduce} region
behaves just as it would under \code{outer}, so a \code{memory}-tag producer may still
legitimately touch ghost cells when writing scratch; only the reduction itself is
confined to the logical set.

\paragraph{Practical notes.} The preferred \code{outer\_reduce} constructs the reducer
over a fresh result and \emph{returns} it; the result is a host scalar, so the reduce
is synchronous and the value is ready on return with no explicit fence. An
escape-hatch overload instead takes a caller-built reducer instance \emph{last}
(matching \code{Kokkos::parallel\_reduce}) for folding into a \code{View},
\code{ScatterView}, or device memory. Reductions are Kokkos-only: they dispatch to the
Kokkos backend regardless of the index space's backend (on a host-only build
\code{DevExecSpace} is the host, so the Kokkos reduce still runs), with no raw path. As
with a plain \code{outer}, the \code{outer\_reduce} body's parameter types must be
named rather than \code{auto} (nvcc forbids \code{auto} parameters on the extended
\verb|__host__ __device__| lambda \code{KOKKOS\_LAMBDA} expands to); use
\code{rist::idx\_range\_t}. The \code{inner\_reduce} body is an ordinary lambda, so its
\code{auto} parameters --- including the trailing accumulator --- are fine.

\section{Contracts and invariants}
\label{sec:contracts}
% =====================================================================

Everything above is only sound because every loop path is held to the same small
set of guarantees. These are the invariants that let a kernel be written once and
trusted under any tag combination. A longer, more granular version of these
contracts is written down in \code{src/loop_abstraction/LOOP_ABSTRACTION_CONTRACTS.md};
be aware, though, that that file is primarily a working document aimed at LLM
assistants, is not guaranteed to be fully current, and contains
process admonitions (e.g.\ ``do not change this without asking'') meant for that
audience. A human reader should treat it as useful background to be taken with a
grain of salt, and defer to the code itself where the two disagree.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Exactly-once coverage.} Every loop pattern touches every logical cell
  exactly once. This is the guarantee a physics kernel depends on: no cell is
  skipped and none is updated twice.
\item \textbf{Logical tags stay inside the logical set.} \code{logical_flat} and
  \code{logical_coords} must not touch ghost cells (outside the deliberate
  extension of a halo range).
\item \textbf{Memory tags may sweep ghosts, but still cover logically.}
  \code{memory} may touch ghost cells that fall inside a contiguous span, but must
  still touch every logical cell exactly once, and the values it leaves in ghost
  cells are not meaningful.
\item \textbf{Backend agreement.} The raw and Kokkos backends produce the same
  logical result for the same loop pattern and body signature. Tests assert this
  directly (raw-vs-Kokkos parity) rather than re-deriving the abstraction's logic.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Body signatures.} As described in Section~\ref{sec:anatomy}, an inner
body may take a single opaque index (\code{auto idx}, the intended form) or
explicit coordinates (\code{int k, int j, int i}, a porting convenience); when both
are viable the three-argument form is selected. Whichever form is used, the
coverage guarantees above hold identically --- the body signature changes only what
the body is handed, not which cells it is called on.

\paragraph{A note on documentation and this abstraction.} The loop abstraction
encodes subtle index-space and scratch contracts, and small changes to the headers
can violate an invariant in ways that do not show up until a specific tag
combination runs. This document is the tutorial companion that motivates why the
machinery is shaped the way it is; the contracts file is a more granular
(LLM-oriented) record of the invariants. Neither is the final word --- the code,
and the unit tests that exercise every tag combination, are the ground truth, and
anyone modifying the headers should lean on those tests to confirm an invariant
still holds.
